About

I got hooked by a protein that falls apart when nothing is holding it.

As an undergraduate at UC Irvine I wrote a proposal to simulate p53, the tumor suppressor mutated in more human cancers than any other protein. It won a small UROP grant, which paid for the work, and I built all fourteen models and ran every simulation myself. The answer was blunt. With DNA bound, the dimer held together. Without it, the two halves rotated apart by roughly 90 degrees. Same protein, same physics; the only variable was what it had hold of. I presented that poster at an ACS national meeting, and I have been chasing versions of the same question ever since.

Proteins move, and most of the software we design drugs with quietly assumes they don't. Closing that gap has been the through-line of everything after. In Ray Luo's lab I worked on binding free energies inside cell membranes, where the standard model treated the whole greasy interior as one uniform material; letting the dielectric change with depth pulled our agreement with measured affinities from an R of 0.59 to 0.91. I also wrote the matrix module that made the underlying Poisson–Boltzmann solve fast enough on GPUs to be worth running. Both shipped in AmberTools, and running across them years later in other groups' papers is still my favorite part of this job. I would rather build something fifty labs use than publish something fifty people read.

At Yale, with Victor Batista, I came at the same problem from several directions. We found a compound that holds the SARS-CoV-2 spike closed from an allosteric pocket instead of competing at the fast-mutating surface every antibody goes for. I mapped how the CRISPR-Cas9 HNH domain switches on, checked against a collaborator's NMR, and found a tyrosine that has to pass through one specific intermediate to get from off to on. For TYK2 I finally tried the thing I had been circling for years: build electron density from simulation alone, with no experimental structure of the complex anywhere in the input, then refine a model into it. It reproduced the crystal structures, and it recovered ordered waters and a second inhibitor conformation the real data had averaged together. Done reliably, that tells you how a molecule binds before anyone synthesizes it.

The machine learning grew out of the same frustration. AlphaFold and most generative design tools hand you one frame of a protein and let you design against it, which is fine right up until selectivity depends on a shape the snapshot never shows. I built a billion-scale screening platform that turned up three new scaffolds against GSK3β, spent a summer at Boehringer Ingelheim teaching models to predict catalyst selectivity, and co-authored the Nature work on an ensemble of specialized LLMs that turns the reaction literature into protocols that actually run.

Today I am a senior scientist at Atombeat, where I started our agentic research group. Most of my week goes into making the computational stack callable by agents, so structure preparation, docking, MD, and FEP finish overnight instead of eating days of somebody's attention, and then putting it to work on a live radioconjugate program against a transmembrane GPCR. The FEP predictions have held to within 0.85 kcal/mol of experiment across about fifty peptides, which is roughly the accuracy you hope for and seldom get. I review for J. Med. Chem. and JCTC, and I take on students whenever there is room. What I want hasn't changed much since the p53 poster: design molecules against the range of shapes a protein actually visits, keep it anchored to experimental data, and put the tools in the hands of people who will aim them at patients' problems rather than mine.
